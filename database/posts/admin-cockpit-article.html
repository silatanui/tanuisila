Admin Cockpit

How I track visitors, posts, and inbox noise without turning the portfolio into a second full-time job

Visit the public site

A portfolio is only half finished when the public pages look good. The other half is the quiet machinery behind them: who arrived, which essay they stayed with, which country the traffic came from, which comment still needs a human decision, and which message is waiting in the inbox. I built an admin cockpit for this site so those answers live in one place, with charts I can read in under a minute and tables I can act on without opening a spreadsheet.

This post is a walkthrough of that cockpit. It is not a SaaS pitch. It is a record of how I manage my own portfolio day to day: unique visitors that do not double-count the same browser, a world map that still shows Hong Kong, pie charts that do not spill titles across the card, and an ops list for comments and mail. The screenshots below are from the live admin UI on local data shaped like real traffic.

The Problem I Wanted to Solve

Early versions of this site were editable, but not operable. I could publish a post, then I had no calm way to see whether anyone read it, whether a campus Wi‑Fi lab looked like one visitor or forty, or whether a kind comment was sitting in moderation while I answered email elsewhere. Analytics tools exist, of course. Most of them are either too heavy for a personal site, too vague about uniqueness, or too disconnected from the content I actually edit.

I wanted a single dashboard that answered five questions without ceremony:

  1. How many distinct people showed up today, this week, and this month?
  2. Where, roughly, are those people coming from?
  3. Which posts are carrying the attention?
  4. Is the portfolio inventory (blog, projects, education, experience, publications) still balanced?
  5. What human work is waiting: unread messages and pending comments?

If the cockpit cannot answer those five, it is decoration. If it can, it becomes part of how I ship.

The First Screen: Numbers Before Narrative

The top of the dashboard is a KPI strip. Unique visitors for today sit next to the seven-day and thirty-day windows. Blog views, unread messages, and pending comments sit beside them. The strip is intentionally boring. Boring is the point. I should be able to glance once and know whether the day is quiet, whether the inbox needs me, and whether moderation is stacking up.

Uniqueness is the hard part hiding under those large numbers. Cookie-only counting inflates when people clear storage or open private windows. IP-only counting collapses a whole campus lab into one person. The compromise I use is a daily hash of IP plus browser family: Chrome on shared Wi‑Fi and Firefox on the same Wi‑Fi can count as two; the same Chrome profile opening incognito does not invent a second person for that day. The meta line under the thirty-day card also shows distinct networks, so I can see when many hashes still share a small set of IPs.

"A dashboard that cannot explain uniqueness is just a vanity counter with better fonts."

Visitors by Country: A Map That Stays Honest

Geography is approximate. An IP is not a passport. Still, a choropleth answers a useful editorial question: is attention local to Kenya and Hungary, or is a post travelling farther than I expected? The map panel shades countries by unique visitors over the last thirty days. Darker means more. The side list shows every located place, not only a vanity top five, because a single visitor from a new country is still a signal worth noticing.

Two map problems showed up immediately and both were design problems disguised as data problems.

First, small places. Hong Kong and Singapore are often missing from the world outline entirely. A shade cannot appear on a shape that is not drawn. When those places have visits, the cockpit drops an orange pin on their coordinates and keeps them in the country list. The map stays a map; the pin is the honesty layer.

Second, legends that lie by omission. A continuous “Fewer → More” bar looks elegant and then fails the moment the top country turns near-black while the legend only shows pale pink and orange. The fix was discrete squared bands that always span the full light-to-dark palette. The top band uses the darkest swatch, and each country is painted with that exact hex. If the United States is the darkest shape on the map, that same dark square sits in the legend with a label like 3 or 50+, depending on the current peak. The bands rebuild from the data: early traffic might read 1, 2, 3; later traffic might read 1–9, 10–49, 50–199, 200+. Compact k and M labels keep the chips readable when the numbers explode.

Charts That Match How I Edit

Under the map, the dashboard splits attention into charts that map to editorial decisions. Unique visitors per day tells me whether a publish day actually moved the needle. Blog readers per day counts distinct devices reading posts inside a twenty-four-hour window per post, which is a tighter story than raw page hits. Content mix shows whether the portfolio inventory is all blog and no publications, or balanced across sections.

The top-posts pie replaced an earlier “blog status” doughnut. Draft versus published is useful on the blog admin table. It is less useful as a dashboard hero. What I actually ask after a launch week is: which essays are absorbing the attention, and in what share? The pie answers that. The legend sits beside the chart as real HTML, not as a cramped canvas footnote, so long titles wrap inside the card instead of drawing a line through the layout.

Lists for Work, Charts for Sense

Charts tell me what is happening. Lists tell me what to do next. Below the chart grid, a split panel shows the top posts by views with a one-click open to the public page, and a feed of recent messages with unread states. That pairing matters. Attention without an inbox is incomplete; an inbox without attention metrics is just email.

The blog admin itself stays list-first. DataTables handle search and sort. The add form collapses until I need it. I want the operating view to be the table of posts, statuses, and views, not a blank form demanding a title every time I open the page.

What a Morning Pass Looks Like

  1. Open the dashboard and read the KPI strip. If unread mail or pending comments are non-zero, those win.
  2. Scan the country map. A new pin or a sudden dark country is a prompt to ask what post might have travelled.
  3. Check unique visitors and blog readers for the last few days. A publish day should leave a mark; if it does not, the issue is discovery or the headline, not the chart.
  4. Look at the top-posts pie. If one essay dominates, decide whether to write a sibling piece or leave it alone.
  5. Only then open the blog table to draft, edit, or schedule.

That order keeps me from polishing a draft while three comments wait for approval. Ops first, craft second.

How the Stack Stays Small

The public site and admin share one PHP and MySQL codebase on ordinary hosting. Visitor rows live in site_daily_visitors with a unique key on visit date and visitor hash. Country fields are filled from Cloudflare headers when present, otherwise from a lightweight IP lookup. Blog views accumulate on the post row for the pie and top list, while a separate view log powers the readers-per-day chart. The dashboard charts use Chart.js. The map uses jsVectorMap with a custom marker list for tiny territories and exact fill colors so the legend never invents a shade the map did not earn.

None of that requires a data warehouse. It requires clear tables, a few honest aggregates, and UI restraint.

How You Can Build a Cockpit Like This

If you want an ops layer on your own portfolio, borrow the questions before you borrow the libraries.

  1. Write the five questions first. Unique people, place, posts, inventory, and human work. If a widget does not answer one of those, cut it.
  2. Decide uniqueness in one sentence. Mine is “IP plus browser family, once per day.” Publish that sentence near the chart so future-you does not reinterpret the number.
  3. Prefer squared legends over pretty gradients. Gradients hide the top shade. Discrete chips that rebuild from the current max stay truthful when traffic is 3 or 30,000.
  4. Pin what the outline cannot show. Tiny territories and missing paths need markers. A list beside the map is not optional; it is the accessibility layer for geography.
  5. Keep chart legends in HTML when labels are long. Canvas legends will truncate, strikethrough, or overflow. Your post titles deserve a column that can wrap.
  6. Make lists the place where work happens. Charts for sense-making, tables for moderation and replies. Collapse create forms until you need them.
  7. Stay on the host you already have. PHP, MySQL, Chart.js, and a vector map are enough. The craft is in the definitions, not in the vendor logo.

What I Left Out on Purpose

There is no real-time websocket firehose. There is no claim that IP geolocation is precise to a neighbourhood. There is no growth hacking panel promising conversion funnels on a personal essay site. I left those out because they would make the cockpit louder without making my decisions better.

I also left out public access to the admin itself. The screenshots are the shareable artifact. The login stays closed. A portfolio can show how it is run without handing strangers the steering wheel.

Admin Cockpit is the ops layer of this portfolio by Tanui Kipngetich Sila. It will not write the next essay for me. It will tell me, quickly and without drama, whether yesterday’s essay found people, where they roughly were, and which human replies still need my name on them.
